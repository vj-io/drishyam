DRISHYAM

Sanskrit: that which is seen

one photo · a few looks · one human

For bridge inspectors reviewing high-resolution photos: it finds cracks, spalling and exposed rebar by zooming in only where it needs to, and hands every critical finding to a human to sign off.

It works like a PTZ camera (pan-tilt-zoom), done in software: it pans and zooms across one big photo instead of moving a real camera.

Try your own photo Watch the video

The US has 624,939 bridges (FHWA, 2026), and the rule is a routine inspection at intervals of up to 24 months (23 CFR 650.311). One drone inspection of a single bridge produced 2,536 photos. Someone has to look at every one. Sources

      HOW IT WORKS

      In one sentence: it looks at the whole photo small, picks the spot most worth a closer look, zooms in when unsure, double-checks what it finds, and stops when the next look is not worth its cost. A watchdog (the governor) enforces the budget, and a person signs off anything critical.

      FIG. 3.1

      Score: where to look next

      Every candidate region gets one number: value per millisecond. The agent looks at the biggest one next.

      score(r) = p · s · (1 + u) · a / t̂

      p
      saliency prior from the overview, 0–1. How likely is something here?
      s
      class severity weight. Exposed rebar 3.0, spalling 2.0, crack 1.5, corrosion 1.0, efflorescence 0.5.
      u
      segmenter entropy at the last level that saw the region, 0 if unseen. How unsure are we?
      a
      fraction of the region not yet seen at the target level. Don’t look twice.
      t̂
      predicted milliseconds for the planned ops, from the live p95. What does looking cost?
      FIG. 3.2

      Five rules

      1. Next gaze. Take the highest score. Ties go to the larger unseen share, then top-left first. That is deterministic for the same timings; live latency changes t̂, so two runs can differ.
      2. Escalate (zoom in). Zoom one pyramid level finer (2×) on the same centre when entropy > τ (0.5), meaning the segmenter is unsure, or the mask is under 64 px, down to L0 (full resolution).
      3. Verify (double-check). When a mask appears at a new level, re-segment one level finer and record the IoU (how much the two masks overlap). Under 0.5 means one more look, or a human for a critical class.
      4. Stop. When the best remaining score falls below λ = ε × (value so far ÷ cost so far), that is, when the next look is worth less than it costs, or any cap is hit.
      5. Governor. The budget-and-speed watchdog runs before every step and may override the model, level or view size. Every override writes a cause into the trace.

      SAFETY AND HUMAN CONTROL

      FIG. 6.1

      Five protections, all in the demo above

      1. Human sign-off. Exposed rebar, and spalling of 40,000 px² or more, are critical. Only a person can approve, reject, close or downgrade them; the work order stays blocked until they do.
      2. KILL. The KILL button tells the server to halt before the next look. The trace records the cause kill-switch and the state is kept, so Resume replays the looks made so far.
      3. Failure drills. Three switches break things on purpose: a slow segmenter (the governor switches to the fast model), a planner that never answers (the rule planner takes over) and a planner that sends a shell command (the allow-list validator rejects it).
      4. Privacy blur. Faces, people and vehicles are found with OpenCV DNN models and blurred before any look is taken. A photo in which one person fills more than a quarter of the frame is refused: this inspects structures, not people.
      5. Signed work orders. The work order is signed with HMAC-SHA256 and every sign-off returns a new signature, so an edited order no longer verifies. The download includes the signature.

      No LLM runs in this demo. The live agent is the rule policy (FIG. 3.2), and the drill planners are scripted to fail. The LLM path is the MCP server: in a recorded session Claude chose each call from the numbers the tools measured (its prompt states the decision rules). Read the recorded session.

      QUESTIONS

      Is this live or recorded?
      Live. The photo goes to the API and every look is computed on the spot with stock OpenCV 5. The page then shows the decisions at reading speed; the agent itself is much faster. The status bar shows the agent’s own time; the upload, decode and privacy blur on the server come on top of it. Watch a recorded run replays a saved job instead, and is labelled recorded.
      What is OpenCV doing here?
      Everything the agent perceives: the image pyramid (pyrDown), the saliency map that picks the candidate regions, the warp that cuts out each look (warpPerspective), the sharpness check (Laplacian), the segmenter that labels damage, and the face and person detectors for the privacy blur (OpenCV DNN). Every decision is made from those numbers.
      Why not just tile the photo?
      Tiling checks every 512 px tile at full resolution: 48 tiles for a 3000 × 2250 photo (turn on “compare with tiling” after a run to see them). The agent looks at the photo small first and zooms only where something might be, so it processes far fewer pixels. Whether it finds as much is measured in Benchmarks: on the synthetic set it finds less than tiling, and one downscaled pass does at least as well.
      What does it cost?
      The $ figures are modelled from wall time at $0.339/h, not billed. A run of a few seconds comes to a small fraction of a cent.
      What are the limits?
      It measures image pixels, not millimetres, because a photo has no scale reference. It is AI-assisted, not an inspection. The sample photos are synthetic; on real CODEBRIM photos the classical segmenter limits recall for every approach (FIG. 4.2). Uploads are capped at 4.4 MB, and bigger photos are shrunk in your browser first.
